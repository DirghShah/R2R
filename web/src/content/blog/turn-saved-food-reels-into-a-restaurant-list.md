The gap between saving a restaurant video and eating at the restaurant is
enormous. Most people cross it a handful of times a year, out of hundreds of
saves. The reason is not laziness — it is that a saved video is missing every
piece of information you need at the moment you decide where to eat.

## What you actually need at decision time

Picture the real moment. It is 7pm, you are with someone, you are somewhere
specific, and you are deciding in under two minutes. What would change your
answer?

- **Is it near me right now?** By far the most important, and the one a saved
  folder cannot answer at all.
- **Is it open?**
- **What kind of food is it?** You are usually in the mood for something.
- **Why did I save it?** The specific dish or detail that made it worth saving.

Notice what is not on that list: the video, the creator, the aesthetic, how many
likes it had. Those are what made you save it. They are not what makes you go.

## The four fields that matter

A restaurant list works when each entry has these. Anything else is decoration.

**1. A location precise enough to navigate to.** Not "Brooklyn". An address, or
better, a pin. This is the field that turns a list into a map, and a map is the
only format that answers "near me right now".

**2. A cuisine or category.** One word. It is how you filter when you know what
you want.

**3. The specific thing.** "The pistachio croissant." "Go before noon, they sell
out." "Cash only." This is the whole reason the recommendation was worth having,
and it is the field most tools drop.

**4. A link back to the source.** Six months later you will not trust your own
note. Being able to re-watch ten seconds of video settles it instantly.

## What to leave out

**Ratings.** You have not been. A rating you invent before visiting is noise, and
it makes the list feel authoritative when it is not. Beli's model — ranking only
places you have actually been — is the right way to do ratings, and it works
precisely because it refuses to rate places you have not visited.

**Elaborate tagging.** Every tag you add is a decision at save time and a filter
you will never use. Cuisine and city are enough.

**Prices.** They change, and short video is unreliable about them.

## Multi-place videos are where lists break

The single most common failure: a video captioned "7 best ramen spots in NYC"
becomes one entry, or none.

This matters more than it sounds, because listicle-format reels are a large share
of what people save — they are the ones that feel most worth keeping. A list that
captures one place out of seven has lost most of its value while appearing to
work.

If you are doing this manually, budget for it: those videos take seven searches,
not one. If you are using a tool, that is the case to test it on before trusting
it with your backlog.

## The test for whether your list is working

Not how many entries it has. **How often it changes where you eat.**

A list of two hundred places you never open is worse than a list of twenty you
check whenever you are deciding — worse because it took longer to build and it
gives you the feeling of being organised without the benefit.

If a month goes by and the list did not decide a single meal, the problem is not
that it needs more entries. It is that it is in the wrong format, missing the
fields that matter, or too slow to open at 7pm with someone waiting.

Fix that, and twenty places will do more for you than four hundred saves ever
did.
